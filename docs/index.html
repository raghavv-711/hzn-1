A chip that listens to the sky.

Every airliner broadcasts who and where it is, several times a second. HZN-1 hears those signals, decodes them, and estimates how much fuel each plane has left. Scroll to take it apart.

Under the lid

Smaller than a fingernail.

The metal lid protects a slab of silicon about 3 mm on a side. Hair-thin gold wires connect it to the package, which plugs into a circuit board through a grid of solder balls.

The floorplan

Eight rooms, one job each.

Like rooms in a house, each area of the chip does one thing: listen to the antenna, decode messages, remember every plane, and estimate its fuel.

      Eleven layers

      A city, stacked eleven floors high.

      Pulled apart, the chip is a stack: switches at the bottom, then five levels of wiring that run like streets, avenues and highways. Tiny pillars called vias connect the floors.

      Down to the switches

      About a million on/off switches.

      Each spot where a red line crosses a green strip is a transistor, about 1/450 the width of a hair. Groups of them add numbers, compare them and remember them. That's all a chip is.

      What it powers

      Every plane in the sky, with fuel left.

      Fuel Horizon puts the chip's output on a globe. Each dot is a real flight from a recording that refreshes every hour, coloured by how much fuel it likely has left. A second mode asks whether it could have made the trip on batteries.
